## Quick answer

Critical Metals (CRML) stock has fallen for three reasons: **(1)** its auditor doubts the company can keep operating for another year, **(2)** up to about **100 million shares** sold to private investors can hit the market whenever the price bounces, and **(3)** the merger with European Lithium grew the share count by about **32%**. All three come back to one problem: **the company doesn't yet have the money to build its Tanbreez rare earth mine in Greenland.**

## My CRML position: down about 80%

I'll be honest about where I stand. I bought Critical Metals at an **average price of $16**, and I still hold it. The stock is now around **$3.02**, so I'm down **roughly 80%**.

I don't have spare cash to average down right now — life has had other plans for my paycheck this year. So I've mentally written this money off and moved on with my life.

But I know some investors are hurting a lot more than I am. So I went through the filings to understand *why* it fell, and whether there's any realistic way out.

## Reason 1: Can the company survive another year?

The company's auditor issued a **going-concern** warning — an opinion that there is substantial doubt the company can keep operating over the next 12 months.

A mining company has no real revenue until the mine is built and selling ore. Until then, it survives by borrowing money or issuing new shares.

The problem is that when investors fear a company might go under *before* the mine is even built, raising money becomes much harder and more expensive. And the market mood has changed: earlier this year rare earths were a hot theme, but now it feels like if you're not an AI stock, nobody wants to sit at your table.

## Reason 2: Shares that can flood the market at any time

Critical Metals didn't raise money through a public offering. It sold shares at a discount to professional investors in **private placements (PIPEs)**.

Those investors are waiting for the price to climb above what they paid so they can take profits. The total that could be sold is **up to about 100 million shares — more than half of all shares outstanding.**

Think of it like a neighborhood where lots of houses are already listed for sale. With that much supply waiting, prices have a hard time going up. Stocks work the same way: every time CRML rallies a little, sellers show up.

## Reason 3: The European Lithium merger added more shares

In November, Critical Metals merged with **European Lithium**, which had been its largest shareholder. European Lithium shareholders were paid in CRML stock — and because the share price had dropped, it took a lot more shares to do the deal.

| Share count | |
|---|---|
| Before the merger | ~147 million |
| After the merger | ~194 million |
| Increase | ~32% |

My first thought was that this might be good news, since the merger brought in cash. And it did: cash more than doubled to about **$264.5 million**.

## Can CRML build the Tanbreez mine with the cash it has?

No. The company's own estimate for building the mine is **at least $400 million, and up to $1 billion**. Even the low end is more than the cash on hand.

And that cash isn't all going into construction. The company has said it will spend this money on studies and permitting, and look for construction financing separately next year. That almost certainly means **more share issuance** — which brings us right back to Reason 2.

There's one more smaller issue. The **letter of interest from the Export-Import Bank of the United States (EXIM)** expired in June. The company has applied to renew it, but nothing is confirmed yet.

That kind of financing matters: it doesn't dilute shareholders, and it signals that the US government sees the project as strategically important. I hope the renewal comes through soon.

## My view: it all comes down to cash

The three reasons CRML keeps falling are really one reason: **cash.** The company still hasn't figured out where the money to build the mine will come from.

Rising interest rates make this worse. When rates go up, investors put less money into companies that are losing money, so the company has to sell new shares at even lower prices.

It hasn't all been bad news, though. There has been real progress, like **approval of the Tanbreez mining plan** and the **first binding offtake (sales) contract**. I'll cover those more positive developments in my next post.

## Correction: the "100% of output pre-sold" claim

I need to correct something I wrote back in January.

I said that **100% of Tanbreez's production had been pre-sold**. That's what the company announced, and I quoted it without much doubt.

After reading the filings, though, only about **15% is covered by a signed, binding contract**. The rest is still at the **non-binding agreement** stage — basically "let's work together on these terms," with no legal obligation behind it.

I'm sorry for the error, and please keep this in mind.

## Key takeaways

- **Price:** CRML is around $3.02; my average cost is $16 (about -80%).
- **Reason 1:** The auditor raised going-concern doubt — the company has no revenue until the mine is built.
- **Reason 2:** Up to ~100 million privately placed shares (over half the total) can be sold into any rally.
- **Reason 3:** The European Lithium merger grew the share count ~32%, from ~147M to ~194M.
- **The real problem:** $264.5M of cash vs. a $400M–$1B mine. More funding, and likely more dilution, is coming.
- **Correction:** Only ~15% of output is under a binding offtake contract, not 100%.
